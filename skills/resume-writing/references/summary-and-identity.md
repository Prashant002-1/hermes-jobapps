# Summary and identity

Load when writing or correcting a summary, a role title, a headline or a project descriptor. Examples are invented.

## Whether to include a summary

Include one when at least one of these is true:

- The history reads as a different lane from the target, and two sentences can reorient the reader.
- The strongest proof is spread across roles and needs one line tying it together.
- The user or the playbook asks for one.

Leave it out when it would only restate employers and tools the page already shows. Those lines do more as a relevant project with a concrete descriptor. On a new resume this is a judgment call each time, not a standing default. If you drop a summary the user has been using, say so; never remove an approved summary during a narrow revision.

## What a summary does

It interprets the evidence. Two or three full sentences, unlabeled or under the heading the source already uses:

1. Durable identity and scope: what kind of practitioner, owning what.
2. Where that has been proven: the current work plus the professional setting.
3. The differentiator: the depth or range most peers lack. Skip this sentence if it has nothing specific to say.

Rules:

- Lead with the identity this employer hires, not the candidate's broadest self-description.
- Complete sentences. No telegraphic fragments ("Builds scalable systems. Ships fast.").
- No adjectives doing the work of evidence: passionate, results-driven, detail-oriented, dynamic, seasoned.
- No skills recap. If a sentence is a list of technologies, it belongs in Skills.
- No objective statement ("Seeking a challenging role where...").
- No years-of-experience count unless the evidence dates support it exactly.

Weak:

> Results-driven software engineer with a passion for building scalable solutions using Python, Go and AWS. Strong communicator and team player seeking new challenges.

Strong:

> Backend engineer who owns services from design through on-call. Built and operates the payment reconciliation path at Northstar and previously shipped billing features inside a forty-engineer codebase. Brings a research habit to production work: measures first, then changes one thing.

## Choosing a title

A role may have more than one truthful title. Choose the one that helps this reader place the work, then keep the full scope in the bullets.

| Target lane | Title for solo or founding work | Summary leads with |
|---|---|---|
| Founder, product leadership, startup operations | Founder | Company and product ownership, outside validation |
| Senior individual-contributor engineering | Founding Engineer | Architecture, production operation, delivery |
| Research, lab or analyst roles | Founding Engineer or the functional title | Reproducible work, analysis, validation |
| Early-career individual contributor | The functional title if truthful | Hands-on execution, outcomes |

Identity-friction check before settling:

1. Will this title help the reviewer understand the work, or make them wonder why the candidate wants this job?
2. Is building a company itself part of what they are hiring for?
3. Does the alternative title keep the ownership truthful and visible?

Guardrails:

- A title the user sets is final. Store it in the playbook under `Resume` so it holds across jobs.
- Never use a title that contradicts the employment relationship, or one the employer could not confirm.
- For employed roles, use the official title. Clarify function in the first bullet if the title is opaque, not by renaming the job.
- Do not erase ownership to make a title safer. Move it into the bullets.

## Project descriptors

Each project line names what the thing is and who it is for before any engineering: a plugin for which platform, an application for which users, a system for which workflow.

- Weak: `Fieldwork | AI workflow automation`
- Strong: `Fieldwork | Scheduling product for independent clinics`

Project names must match what the reader can find: the portfolio, the repository, the link on the page.

## Correcting a summary

1. Read two or three of the candidate's earlier approved summaries in other job folders.
2. Keep the strongest sentence skeleton and its ownership language. Do not replace it with a weaker compressed identity.
3. Change only what was asked.
4. Once wording is approved, it is frozen. A later layout repair must not alter it. If the summary's last line wraps badly, fix the break after approval with a line-level adjustment, not a rewrite.

## Headline in the profile

`profile/profile.md` may carry a `headline`. Treat it as the default identity for general documents. A tailored resume may use a different truthful identity for its lane; do not write that back to the profile unless the user asks.
