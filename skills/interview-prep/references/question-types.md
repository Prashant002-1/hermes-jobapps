# Question types and answer outlines

Load when drafting likely questions for the pack or practicing a specific round. Questions listed in a pack are predictions from the posting and the submitted documents. Label them as such unless the employer supplied the format.

## How to predict questions

1. From the submitted resume: every bullet invites "tell me more about that" and "what was your part?". Every number invites "how was that measured?".
2. From the posting: each core responsibility becomes "tell me about a time you..." and "how would you...".
3. From the gap between them: whatever the posting asks for and the resume shows least. Expect it early.
4. From the round: see the sections below.

List eight to twelve questions for a round. For each, write the story or evidence to use and three or four spoken beats. Not a script.

## Recruiter screen

Likely: "Walk me through your background", "Why this role and why now?", "What are you looking for?", "What is your timeline?", salary expectations, authorization, location and start date.

- The recruiter needs a short, positive handoff for the hiring manager: what this person has done, why they want this, that they communicate clearly, that logistics work.
- Answers are short. Two minutes is long here.
- Salary: use the profile's range. If the user prefers, ask for the role's range first, politely, once. Never invent a number in the moment; agree it beforehand.
- Authorization: the true answer to the question asked, briefly. Prepared wording comes from the profile.
- Ask about the process: stages, timeline, who the next conversation is with.

## Hiring manager

Likely: "Tell me about the project you are proudest of", "What was the hardest decision in it?", "How do you prioritize?", "Tell me about a disagreement", "What would you do in your first ninety days?", "What do you want to learn?".

- The manager is picturing the candidate on the team. Use stories with ownership and judgment.
- "First ninety days": learn the system and the people, find one small thing to ship early, then a larger one. Tie each to something verified about the team. Do not prescribe fixes to a system not yet seen.
- Bring real questions about the work: what the team is stuck on, how decisions get made, what a strong first six months looks like.

## Technical: coding

- Confirm the format in advance: live editor, own environment, language choices, whether running code is allowed.
- Method to practice aloud: restate the problem, ask about inputs and edge cases, give a simple approach first, state its cost, improve it, write it, test with a small example, name what was not handled.
- Silence is the common failure. Narrate decisions, not keystrokes.
- When stuck: say what is known, try a smaller case, ask a targeted question. Asking is not failing.
- Review the fundamentals the posting implies and everything the resume claims. A claimed language is fair game at depth.

## Technical: deep dive on past work

Likely: "Draw the architecture", "Why that design?", "What broke?", "What would you do differently at ten times the scale?".

- Prepare one system the candidate knows completely. Be ready to draw it, name each component's reason for existing, and state two decisions with the alternative rejected.
- Know the numbers: load, data size, latency, cost, team size. If a number is unknown, say so. Do not invent one in the room.
- Keep "I" and "we" exact. Expect "what was your part?".

## System design

Method for the first five minutes: clarify who uses it and what must be true, state the scale assumptions aloud, name the two or three hardest requirements, sketch the simplest design that meets them, then deepen where the interviewer steers.

- Offer trade-offs unprompted: what this design gives up, when a different choice would be right.
- Use the candidate's own systems as reference points: "we hit this at smaller scale and chose X because...".
- For early-career candidates the bar is structured reasoning, not production war stories. Say what is known from experience and what is reasoned from principles.
- Practise two designs drawn from the employer's domain.

## Behavioral

Likely stems: "Tell me about a time you... disagreed with a teammate, missed a deadline, failed, handled unclear requirements, influenced without authority, received hard feedback, dealt with a difficult customer".

- Each gets one story from the bank, told in beats, about two minutes.
- Answer the stem asked. If the story is about a disagreement, the disagreement is in the first twenty seconds.
- End on the consequence and the lesson in one sentence. Do not trail off into general principles.
- If no true story exists for a stem, say so and offer the nearest real one. Do not invent.
- Hypotheticals ("what would you do if...") get an answer, then a real example of having done something like it.

## Take-home

- Read the brief twice. List what is required, what is a stretch, and what is unstated. Ask the employer one clarifying message if something material is ambiguous.
- Fix the time budget they state and honour it. Record the hours spent.
- Finish the required part cleanly before any stretch. A small complete solution with tests and a clear README beats a large half-working one.
- Include a short written rationale: decisions, trade-offs, what was left out and why, what would come next.
- The work must be the candidate's own. The agent can review, question and explain, and can help with what the brief permits. If the brief restricts AI assistance, follow it.
- Prepare for the follow-up conversation: be ready to change the solution live and to defend each decision.

## Onsite or panel

- Map interviewers to likely focus by role, and assign different stories so nobody hears the same one twice.
- Prepare one question specific to each interviewer.
- Consistency matters: the same facts, the same numbers, the same reasons for wanting the role.
- Confirm the schedule and who is in each slot, so stories can be assigned in advance.

## Questions that need prepared wording

Prepare each once. State the fact, then move to the relevant strength.

| Question | Shape of the answer |
|---|---|
| "Why are you leaving?" or "Why did you leave?" | What the candidate is moving toward. Nothing negative about a past employer. |
| "Explain this gap." | What happened, in one sentence. What was done in that time if relevant. Back to the present. |
| "You have not done X." | Agree where true, once. The closest real mechanism. How the gap would be closed. |
| "Why this lane, given your background?" | The pull toward the work, the evidence it is already acted on, what the background adds. |
| "Where do you see yourself in five years?" | A direction, not a title. Consistent with the role. |
| "What is your biggest weakness?" | See the story bank reference. |
| "Do you have other offers?" or timeline questions | The truth, briefly. Never bluff an offer. |

## Questions to ask, by interviewer

| Interviewer | Good territory | Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Recruiter | Process and timeline, what the team is prioritizing in this hire, how the role came to be open | Internal architecture |
| Hiring manager | What the team is stuck on, how success is judged in six months, how decisions get made | Anything answered in the posting |
| Peer | A recent piece of work, what on-call or review is really like, what they wish they had known | Compensation |
| Senior leader | Where the team's work fits the company's direction, what would make them worried in a year | Details of daily work |

Always have a next-steps question ready: "What happens after this conversation, and when should I expect to hear?"
